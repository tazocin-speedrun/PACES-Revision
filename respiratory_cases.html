Case 01 · Most Common · 21.4%

Interstitial Lung Disease / Pulmonary Fibrosis

21.4% — most common Station 1 Stem: dyspnoea / cough / incidental finding
⚡ Spot diagnosis — before you touch the patient
ClubbingFine bibasal crackles (Velcro) Central cyanosis possibleCushingoid (steroids) Look for CTD signs — RA hands, scleroderma, SLE rash
🔎Full examination — findings▶
1
End of bed
Breathless at rest? RR? Cushingoid (steroid treatment). Look for CTD signs — RA hands, scleroderma facies, butterfly rash (SLE). Oxygen at home? Inhalers?
2
Hands
Clubbing — present in UIP/IPF, asbestosis. Absent in sarcoidosis, HP, drug-induced. RA changes, scleroderma, vasculitic changes. CO₂ retention flap (asterixis) if severe.
3
Face / eyes
Central cyanosis. Cushingoid. Scleroderma (beaked nose, microstomia, telangiectasia). Lupus rash. Anaemia.
4
Neck / lymph nodes
Trachea — central in ILD. Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (sarcoidosis, malignancy). JVP — raised if cor pulmonale.
5
Chest inspection
No scars expected in uncomplicated ILD. Small volume chest (reduced lung volumes). Reduced chest wall movement.
6
Expansion / percussion
Reduced expansion bilaterally. Percussion — resonant or mildly dull at bases (not stony dull — no effusion).
7
Auscultation + vocal resonance
Fine end-inspiratory crackles, bibasal — like Velcro, do NOT change with coughing (distinguishes from bronchiectasis). Normal vocal resonance. No wheeze.
8
Completions
Ankle oedema (cor pulmonale). Parasternal heave + loud P2 (pulmonary HTN — severity marker). SpO₂. Check for hepatomegaly (sarcoidosis).
💬Ideal presentation script▶
1 — Diagnosis
"I think this patient has pulmonary fibrosis."
Unless a specific cause is identifiable from examination findings, use the clinical descriptor "pulmonary fibrosis" pending investigation. State the likely aetiology if CTD signs are present.
2 — Positive signs
"The positive signs are: digital clubbing, fine end-inspiratory crackles at both bases that do not change with coughing — consistent with Velcro crackles of fibrosis — and reduced bilateral chest expansion. The patient appears [comfortable at rest / breathless at rest — suggesting significant disease]."
If CTD signs present: "There are features of [RA / systemic sclerosis / SLE] suggesting a connective tissue disease aetiology."
3 — Important negatives (fixed safety screen)
"Regarding respiratory effort: the patient is [comfortable / using accessory muscles] at rest with a RR of approximately [X]. There is no finger clubbing [or: clubbing is present — noted above]. There are no signs of cor pulmonale — JVP is not raised, there is no ankle oedema, no parasternal heave, and P2 is not accentuated."
4 — Of note
"Of note, the patient has Cushingoid features consistent with systemic steroid therapy. There are no signs of malignancy — no cervical lymphadenopathy, no Horner's syndrome."

🔬Investigations and management▶
Investigations
CXR — reticulonodular changes, small lungs, loss of cardiac border definition
HRCT — UIP pattern: bibasal subpleural honeycombing; ground glass = NSIP (CTD)
PFTs — restrictive: FEV₁/FVC >0.8, low TLC, reduced TLco and KCO
ABG — type I respiratory failure
Bloods — ESR, RF, ANA, ANCA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Scl-70, anti-CCP
BAL — exclude infection pre-immunosuppression; lymphocytes >neutrophils = better steroid response
Lung biopsy — if HRCT inconclusive (~7% morbidity)
Management
IPF: pirfenidone (antifibrotic) — FEV₁ 50–80% predicted (NICE)
IPF: nintedanib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) — alternative antifibrotic
CTD-ILD: immunosuppression (steroids, azathioprine, mycophenolate)
Avoid steroids + azathioprine in IPF (PANTHER trial — increased mortality)
Pulmonary rehab, O₂ if hypoxic
Single lung transplant for selected patients
Prognosis: UIP/IPF — 80% 5yr mortality; NSIP (CTD) — 80% 5yr survival
🎓Examiner questions▶
What is the difference between UIP and NSIP on HRCT?
UIP (usual interstitial pneumonia): bibasal subpleural honeycombing — typical of IPF. No response to immunosuppression. NSIP (non-specific interstitial pneumonia): widespread ground glass shadowing — associated with CTD. Better response to immunosuppression and better prognosis.
Why is clubbing present in IPF but not sarcoidosis?
Clubbing is associated with chronic hypoxia and fibrotic lung disease with honeycombing. In sarcoidosis the fibrosis pattern is different (upper lobe, lymphangitic) and clubbing is not a feature. Its presence in ILD suggests UIP/IPF, asbestosis, or CTD-ILD rather than sarcoidosis or HP.
What is the significance of the crackles not changing with coughing?
Fine end-inspiratory crackles that do not change with coughing are characteristic of fibrosis — they arise from the sudden opening of stiff, fibrosed airways. Crackles that change or clear with coughing suggest secretions — as in bronchiectasis or COPD.
What does a low KCO indicate?
KCO (transfer coefficient) reflects alveolar gas exchange efficiency. Low KCO = intrinsic parenchymal disease (fibrosis, emphysema). In ILD, both TLco and KCO are reduced. In anaemia, TLco is low but KCO is normal (less haemoglobin to carry CO, not a lung problem).
What are the causes of apical fibrosis?
TRASHE: TB, Radiation, Ankylosing spondylitis / ABPA, Sarcoidosis, Histoplasmosis / Histiocytosis X, Extrinsic allergic alveolitis (hypersensitivity pneumonitis). Basal fibrosis: UIP/IPF, asbestosis, CTD, aspiration.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Saying "pulmonary fibrosis" without looking for an underlying cause — always examine hands, face, and skin for CTD
Confusing ILD crackles (fine, end-inspiratory, fixed) with bronchiectasis crackles (coarse, mixed, change with coughing)
Missing clubbing — examine carefully; its presence or absence significantly narrows the differential
Not checking for cor pulmonale — parasternal heave and loud P2 are key severity markers
Describing percussion as dull — fibrosis gives reduced resonance but not stony dullness; that suggests effusion

Lobectomy

⚡ Spot diagnosis

🔎Full examination — findings▶
1
End of bed
Cachexia (malignancy — most common cause). Tar-stained fingers. Breathless? Cushingoid (post-chemotherapy steroids). Look for VATS port sites (minimally invasive).
2
Hands
Clubbing (malignancy, bronchiectasis). Tar staining. CO₂ retention flap. Peripheral cyanosis.
3
Face / neck
Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) — apical tumour (Pancoast). Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy. Central cyanosis.
4
Trachea
Central — key distinction from pneumonectomy (where trachea deviates toward affected side). Small deviation toward resected side may be present but minimal.
5
Chest inspection
Lateral thoracotomy scar — same appearance for upper or lower lobectomy. VATS port sites (3 small scars). Chest wall deformity / asymmetry on affected side.
6
Expansion / percussion
Reduced expansion ipsilateral side. Lower lobectomy: dull percussion lower zone, absent breath sounds. Upper lobectomy: may be hyperresonant upper zone, dull at base (elevated hemidiaphragm).
7
Auscultation + vocal resonance
Absent or reduced breath sounds over resected lobe. Remaining lung may have compensatory increased breath sounds. Check contralateral lung — metastases / synchronous disease.
8
Completions
Ankle oedema (cor pulmonale). Check for radiation tattoo marks. Hepatomegaly (metastases). SpO₂.
💬Presentation script▶
1
"I think this patient has had a [right / left] [upper / lower] lobectomy."
2
"The positive signs are: a lateral thoracotomy scar on the [right / left], reduced expansion on the same side, a [dull percussion note over the lower zone / hyperresonant upper zone] with absent breath sounds over the resected lobe, and a centrally placed trachea. The underlying aetiology appears to be [bronchial carcinoma — given the cachexia and clubbing / previous TB / bronchiectasis]."
3
"Regarding respiratory effort: the patient is [comfortable / breathless] at rest. [Clubbing is present — raises concern for malignancy or bronchiectasis / There is no clubbing]. There are no signs of cor pulmonale — JVP not raised, no ankle oedema, no RV heave, P2 not accentuated."
4
"Of note, there is [no Horner's syndrome / no supraclavicular lymphadenopathy] — no evidence of residual or recurrent malignant disease on examination."

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you distinguish upper from lower lobectomy?
Lower lobectomy: dull percussion lower zone, absent breath sounds at base — most straightforward. Upper lobectomy: may have hyperresonant upper zone (air-filled space) with dull base (elevated hemidiaphragm). Trachea is central in both.
How do you distinguish lobectomy from pneumonectomy?
Pneumonectomy: trachea deviates toward the affected side, dull throughout the entire hemithorax, absent TVF throughout, bronchial breathing upper zone. Lobectomy: trachea central, dullness limited to resected zone only.
What are the indications for lobectomy in lung cancer?
NSCLC resectable disease — T1-2N0-1M0. FEV₁ must be adequate — pneumonectomy contraindicated if FEV₁ <1.2L. Pre-operatively: CT staging, PET scan, mediastinoscopy / EBUS to exclude N2 disease. Multidisciplinary team decision.
What is a Pancoast tumour?
Apical lung tumour invading the superior sulcus. Causes Horner's syndrome (sympathetic chain involvement: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis), shoulder/arm pain (brachial plexus), and rib destruction. Treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery if resectable.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Confusing lobectomy with pneumonectomy — tracheal position is the key differentiator
Not commenting on the underlying cause — always look for malignancy signs (cachexia, clubbing, Horner's, lymphadenopathy)
Not examining the contralateral lung — synchronous tumours and metastases occur
Forgetting VATS port sites — three small scars may be the only evidence of surgery

🔎Full examination — findings▶

End of bed
Sputum pot — look in it (purulent, copious). Cachexia, tachypnoea. Cystic fibrosis features if young (small stature, Portacath, PEG). RA hands (associated cause).
2
Hands
Clubbing — present in established bronchiectasis. Peripheral cyanosis. RA changes.
3

Central cyanosis. Yellow nail syndrome (yellow nails + lymphoedema — associated). Sinus disease (sinusitis — Kartagener's). Nasal polyps.
4
Trachea
Usually central unless fibrosis / collapse from chronic disease.
5
Chest / expansion
May be hyperinflated in severe disease. Reduced expansion if extensive. No typical scar.
6
Percussion
Usually resonant. Dull over areas of consolidation or collapse. Not stony dull.
7
Auscultation ★
Coarse mixed crackles — change with coughing (secretions in dilated airways). Occasional wheeze and squeaks. Widespread or localised depending on extent. Bilateral lower lobe predominance common.
8
Completions
Ankle oedema, RV heave, loud P2 (cor pulmonale — late complication). Dip urine for protein (secondary amyloidosis). SpO₂.
💬Presentation script▶
1
"I think this patient has bronchiectasis."
2
"The positive signs are: digital clubbing, a sputum pot at the bedside containing purulent secretions, and coarse crackles on auscultation that change in character with coughing, consistent with secretions in dilated airways. There are occasional expiratory squeaks."
If cause identifiable: "The underlying aetiology may be [cystic fibrosis — given the young age, small stature and Portacath / RA — given the joint changes / Kartagener's — given the situs inversus]."
3
"Regarding respiratory effort: the patient is [comfortable / tachypnoeic] at rest. Clubbing is present — as noted. There are no signs of cor pulmonale — JVP not raised, no ankle oedema, no RV heave, P2 not accentuated."
🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the causes of bronchiectasis?
Congenital: cystic fibrosis, Kartagener's syndrome (primary ciliary dyskinesia + situs inversus). Childhood infection: measles, TB, whooping cough. Immune overactivity: ABPA (upper lobe), IBD. Immune underactivity: hypogammaglobulinaemia, CVID. Aspiration: localised to right lower lobe. Post-obstructive (tumour, foreign body).
How does bronchiectasis appear on HRCT?
Signet ring sign — thickened, dilated bronchus larger than the adjacent vascular bundle. Tramlines on CXR. Ring shadows (end-on dilated bronchi). Mucus plugging.
What are the complications of bronchiectasis?
Cor pulmonale (late). Secondary amyloidosis (check urine for protein). Massive haemoptysis (mycotic aneurysm — bronchial artery). Recurrent pneumonia. Respiratory failure.
How do you distinguish bronchiectasis from COPD on examination?
Bronchiectasis: coarse crackles changing with coughing, sputum pot, clubbing, usually younger. COPD: expiratory polyphonic wheeze, hyperinflation, barrel chest, no clubbing, tar staining, pursed lips. Both can coexist.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Not looking in the sputum pot — if it is there it is a deliberate clue
Confusing coarse crackles (bronchiectasis — change with coughing) with fine crackles (ILD — fixed)
Not looking for the underlying cause — cystic fibrosis, Kartagener's, RA, hypogammaglobulinaemia
Missing yellow nail syndrome — yellow nails + lymphoedema is a specific association

COPD

⚡ Spot diagnosis

🔎Full examination — findings▶
1

Pursed-lip breathing. Inhalers, nebuliser, O₂ (LTOT — 15hrs/day). Breathless at rest. Cachexia. Tar-stained fingers. CO₂ retention flap (asterixis — bounding pulse).
2

Tar staining. No clubbing — COPD does not cause clubbing. If clubbing is present consider bronchial carcinoma or bronchiectasis. CO₂ retention flap. Bounding pulse (CO₂ retention).

4

Central. Tracheal tug (severe hyperinflation).
5
Chest inspection
Barrel chest — increased AP diameter. Hyperinflated. Reduced chest wall movement.
6
Expansion / percussion
Reduced expansion bilaterally. Hyperresonant throughout — loss of cardiac dullness. Liver dullness displaced downward.
7
Auscultation
Expiratory polyphonic wheeze. Reduced breath sounds at apices (emphysema — air trapping, loss of alveoli). Prolonged expiratory phase. Crackles if concurrent consolidation.
8
Completions
JVP, ankle oedema, RV heave, loud P2 (cor pulmonale — raised JVP, oedema, pansystolic murmur of TR). SpO₂. BP.

1
"I think this patient has COPD."
2
"The positive signs are: pursed-lip breathing and use of accessory muscles at rest, a barrel chest with increased AP diameter, hyperresonant percussion throughout with loss of cardiac dullness, reduced breath sounds at the apices, and an expiratory polyphonic wheeze. 3

There is no finger clubbing — COPD does not cause clubbing; its presence would prompt consideration of malignancy or bronchiectasis. There are [no signs / signs] of cor pulmonale — [JVP not raised, no ankle oedema / raised JVP and ankle oedema present], [no / confirmed] RV heave, P2 [not accentuated / accentuated]."

🎓Examiner questions▶
GOLD classification of COPD?
Based on post-bronchodilator FEV₁: GOLD 1 (mild) ≥80%, GOLD 2 (moderate) 50–79%, GOLD 3 (severe) 30–49%, GOLD 4 (very severe) <30%. Also incorporates symptoms (CAT score / mMRC dyspnoea scale) and exacerbation history for management grouping (A–D).
What are the criteria for LTOT?
Non-smoker. PaO₂ <7.3kPa on air (or <8kPa if cor pulmonale present). PaCO₂ that does not rise excessively on O₂. Administered at 2–4L/min via nasal prongs for ≥15 hours/day. Improves survival by approximately 9 months.
Why does COPD not cause clubbing?
Clubbing requires a specific trigger — thought to be related to megakaryocyte fragments bypassing the pulmonary vasculature and causing periosteal changes. COPD causes airflow obstruction and hyperinflation but does not trigger this mechanism. Clubbing in a COPD patient should prompt urgent exclusion of bronchial carcinoma.
What is the single most beneficial management in COPD?
Smoking cessation — it is the only intervention proven to slow the decline in FEV₁. LTOT improves survival in hypoxic patients. Pulmonary rehabilitation improves exercise capacity and quality of life.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Not commenting that COPD does not cause clubbing — always flag this explicitly if asked
Missing cor pulmonale — check JVP, ankles, RV heave, P2 in every COPD case
Calling wheeze monophonic — COPD gives polyphonic wheeze (multiple airways); monophonic suggests a single obstructed airway (tumour, foreign body)
Not noting the equipment at the bedside — inhalers, nebuliser, O₂ cylinders are all clues

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you differentiate effusion from consolidation on examination?
Both: dull percussion, reduced breath sounds. Key differences — Effusion: stony dull (consolidation is just dull), absent TVF (consolidation has increased TVF / VR), absent breath sounds (consolidation has bronchial breathing). Trachea pushed away in large effusion (consolidation — central or toward if with collapse).
What are the features of an empyema on aspiration?
Pus on aspiration. Low glucose, pH <7.2. Organisms on Gram stain / culture. Anaerobes, staphylococci, Gram-negative organisms most common. Requires drainage — chest drain or surgical decortication if loculated.
How do you manage a malignant pleural effusion?
Symptomatic aspiration (thoracocentesis) for relief. Chemical pleurodesis with talc if recurrent — prevents re-accumulation. Indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) for palliation in poor performance status. Treat underlying malignancy where possible.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Calling the percussion note "dull" rather than "stony dull" — stony dullness is specific to effusion
Not assessing TVF — its absence is essential to confirm effusion vs consolidation
Forgetting to comment on the likely cause — the examiner will always ask; pre-empt it
Describing tracheal deviation toward the effusion — trachea is pushed away or central, never toward

🎓Examiner questions▶
What fills the pneumonectomy space?
Initially air and fluid. Over several weeks the space fills with gelatinous fibrotic material. This explains the absent TVF (no lung tissue to conduct vibrations) and the dull percussion throughout.
What are the contraindications to pneumonectomy?
FEV₁ <1.2L (post-operative predicted). Contralateral lung disease. Poor cardiac function. N2 (mediastinal) nodal disease on staging. Age and performance status are relative contraindications. Pre-operative assessment includes V/Q scan to predict post-pneumonectomy FEV₁.

3
"Regarding respiratory effort: the patient is [comfortable / using accessory muscles / breathless] at rest — [suggesting well-compensated / significant] disease. There is no finger clubbing. There are [no signs / signs] of cor pulmonale — [JVP / ankle oedema / RV heave]."
🎓Examiner questions▶
How does kyphoscoliosis cause respiratory failure?
Chest wall deformity reduces thoracic compliance — the increased work of breathing leads to hypoventilation. Type II respiratory failure results (hypoxia + hypercapnia). During sleep, respiratory muscle fatigue worsens. Cor pulmonale from chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is a late complication.
What are the causes of kyphoscoliosis?
Idiopathic (most common — adolescent girls). Neuromuscular: polio, cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, neurofibromatosis. Connective tissue: Marfan's, Ehlers-Danlos. Congenital vertebral abnormalities. Osteoporosis (kyphosis alone — vertebral fractures).
What PFT pattern does kyphoscoliosis give?
Restrictive: FEV₁/FVC normal or elevated (>0.8), low TLC, low VC. TLco may be relatively preserved (lung parenchyma intact). This distinguishes it from ILD (low TLco) and COPD (obstructive pattern).
✗Common pitfalls▶
Attributing tracheal deviation to pathological disease — it reflects the structural deformity
Not looking for an underlying cause — always inspect the skin (café-au-lait spots) and assess habitus (Marfan's)
Not checking for cor pulmonale — hypoventilation causes pulmonary HTN and eventual RV failure

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you assess severity of an acute asthma attack?
Moderate: PEFR 50–75% best, no severe features. Severe: PEFR 33–50%, RR ≥25, HR ≥110, unable to complete sentences. Life-threatening: PEFR <33%, SpO₂ <92%, silent chest, cyanosis, bradycardia, exhaustion, altered consciousness. Near-fatal: raised PaCO₂ requiring ventilation.
How does asthma differ from COPD on examination?
Asthma: younger, atopic features, no clubbing, no tar staining, episodic — may be normal between attacks, significant reversibility on spirometry (>15% FEV₁ change). COPD: older, smoker, tar staining, barrel chest, CO₂ retention, no reversibility, cor pulmonale in severe disease.
What is the BTS stepwise approach to asthma management?
Step 1: SABA PRN. Step 2: add inhaled corticosteroid (ICS). Step 3: add LABA (if LABA ineffective, stop and try LTRA or theophylline). Step 4: increase ICS dose, add LTRA / theophylline. Step 5: add oral corticosteroids, refer to specialist, consider biologics (e.g. omalizumab for severe allergic asthma, mepolizumab for severe eosinophilic).
✗Common pitfalls▶
Not noting the inhalers at the bedside — they are a deliberate clue
Not distinguishing polyphonic from monophonic wheeze — polyphonic = diffuse airway disease; monophonic = single obstructed airway
Forgetting that silent chest is more severe, not better — absence of wheeze in a distressed patient is a red flag
Attributing clubbing to asthma — it does not cause clubbing; if present, reconsider the diagnosis

🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the pulmonary manifestations of RA?
ILD (most common — NSIP or UIP pattern). Pleural effusion (exudate — low glucose characteristic). Obliterative bronchiolitis. Pulmonary nodules (may cavitate). Caplan's syndrome (nodules + coal dust). Drug-induced ILD (methotrexate, leflunomide, gold). Pulmonary HTN (rare).
How does RA-ILD differ from IPF prognostically?
RA-ILD is usually NSIP pattern — better prognosis than UIP/IPF. Responds better to immunosuppression. However, drug-induced ILD from DMARDs must first be excluded by stopping the offending drug and reassessing. RA-ILD carries 5yr mortality significantly lower than IPF.
What is the characteristic feature of RA pleural effusion on aspiration?
Very low glucose (<1.6 mmol/L) — characteristic of RA effusion. Also: low pH, high LDH, high protein (exudate). Rheumatoid factor may be positive in effusion. Cholesterol crystals on microscopy (pseudochylothorax in chronic RA effusion).

TypeScarIndications ("dry" vs "wet")
Single lungLateral thoracotomyCOPD, IPF/pulmonary fibrosis — "dry lung" conditions
Bilateral / doubleClamshell (transverse)CF, bronchiectasis, pulmonary HTN — "wet lung" conditions (bilateral needed to prevent contamination from native lung)

🎓Examiner questions▶
What is obliterative bronchiolitis and how does it present?
The most important late complication of lung transplantation — chronic rejection affecting small airways. Pathologically: fibroproliferative obliteration of bronchioles. Clinically: progressive dyspnoea, wheeze, declining FEV₁. Called bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) clinically. Managed with increased immunosuppression (azithromycin, tacrolimus adjustment).
What immunosuppression regimen is used post-lung transplant?
Triple therapy: calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or ciclosporin) + antiproliferative agent (mycophenolate or azathioprine) + corticosteroid (prednisolone). Side effects: tacrolimus — nephrotoxicity, tremor, hypertension, neurotoxicity. Ciclosporin — gum hypertrophy, hirsutism, nephrotoxicity. Steroids — Cushingoid features, osteoporosis, diabetes.
What is the 5-year survival following lung transplant?
Approximately 50% at 5 years — lower than heart or kidney transplant. Limited by obliterative bronchiolitis (chronic rejection) and opportunistic infections. CF transplant outcomes are among the best for lung transplant indications.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Missing the clamshell scar — it runs transversely across the lower chest and is easily overlooked if not specifically looked for
Confusing single lung transplant scar with lobectomy / pneumonectomy — the key is the normal examination on the transplant side
Not identifying the original disease — the examiner will always ask; use the habitus, age, and contralateral findings
Not commenting on immunosuppression signs — Cushingoid features, skin changes, gum hypertrophy are all part of the picture
Missing wheeze on the transplant side — this is the cardinal sign of obliterative bronchiolitis / chronic rejection
