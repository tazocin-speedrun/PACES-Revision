🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the top three causes of renal transplantation?
Glomerulonephritis (most common overall), diabetic nephropathy (most common in older patients, rising in frequency), and ADPKD. Other causes include hypertensive nephropathy, systemic sclerosis, RA, and amyloidosis.
How do you distinguish acute from chronic rejection?
Acute rejection (weeks to months): rising creatinine, graft tenderness, fever, oliguria. Treat with IV methylprednisolone; resistant cases need anti-thymocyte globulin. Chronic rejection: gradual creatinine rise over months to years, proteinuria, hypertension — chronic alloimmune injury. Limited treatment; eventual graft failure.
What are the side effects of the main immunosuppressants?
Tacrolimus: nephrotoxicity, fine tremor, hypertension, diabetes. Ciclosporin: nephrotoxicity, gum hypertrophy, hirsutism, hypertension. Mycophenolate: GI upset, myelosuppression. Azathioprine: myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity. Prednisolone: Cushingoid, osteoporosis, diabetes, hypertension, cataracts.
Why is the AV fistula preferred over other HD access?
Native AV fistula (radiocephalic at wrist or brachiocephalic at elbow) has lowest infection risk, longest patency, and highest flow rates. Tunnelled central venous catheters have highest infection risk. "Fistula first" — creation should precede need by months to allow maturation.
What skin malignancies are increased after transplant and why?
SCC ×100, BCC ×10 — unlike general population where BCC > SCC, transplant patients have SCC predominance with more aggressive behaviour. Caused by impaired immune surveillance of UV-induced DNA damage. Annual dermatology review, sun protection advice, prompt treatment of actinic keratoses.
How do you assess fluid status in a transplant patient and why does it matter?
JVP, peripheral oedema, BP, skin turgor, mucous membranes. Overload: hypertension and graft strain. Depletion: pre-renal insult triggering rejection or ATN. Optimal euvolaemia is critical — especially early post-transplant.
What is PTLD?
Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease — EBV-driven B-cell proliferation in impaired T-cell immunosurveillance. Ranges from benign polyclonal to aggressive monomorphic lymphoma. Presents with lymphadenopathy, fever, weight loss, organ infiltration. Treatment: reduce immunosuppression ± rituximab ± chemotherapy.
What is the significance of gum hypertrophy?
Indicates ciclosporin use. Also caused by phenytoin, nifedipine, and pregnancy. In transplant patients, alerts to ciclosporin nephrotoxicity as a cause of declining graft function — ciclosporin levels must always be monitored alongside this finding.

🎓Examiner questions▶
What causes of CLD can you identify on examination?
Alcohol: Dupuytren's, parotid swelling, peripheral neuropathy, multiple bruises. Viral hepatitis B/C: tattoos, needle marks, nasal septal perforation. PBC: xanthelasma, middle-aged woman, scratch marks from pruritus. Haemochromatosis: slate-grey pigmentation, injection sites (diabetes), 2nd/3rd MCP arthropathy. Wilson's: Kayser-Fleischer rings (slit lamp), young patient, neurological signs. AIH: Cushingoid (on steroids), young-middle-aged woman.
What are the signs of decompensation?
Jaundice (failing synthetic function). Ascites (portal HTN + hypoalbuminaemia + secondary hyperaldosteronism). Encephalopathy/asterixis (nitrogenous compounds bypassing hepatic metabolism). Coagulopathy/bruising (failure to synthesise clotting factors). SBP — always aspirate new ascites. These features define decompensated cirrhosis.
What are the autoantibodies in liver disease?
PBC: AMA-M2 in 98%, elevated IgM. PSC: p-ANCA in some. AIH type 1: anti-smooth muscle (ASMA), ANA. AIH type 2: anti-LKM1. Pattern of autoantibodies classifies AIH type and guides treatment decisions.
What is the management of ascites in cirrhosis?
Abstinence (alcohol). Salt restriction (<5g/day). Diuretics: spironolactone first-line, add furosemide if needed. Aim 1kg/day max weight loss. Tense ascites: therapeutic paracentesis + albumin (8g/litre drained). TIPS for refractory ascites. Liver transplant for end-stage disease.
What is SBP and how is it diagnosed and treated?
Infection of ascitic fluid without a surgically treatable source — bacterial translocation in cirrhotic ascites. Organisms: E. coli, Klebsiella, Strep pneumoniae. Diagnosed by ascitic tap: neutrophil count >250 cells/mm³. Treat with IV cefotaxime + albumin infusion (reduces HRS risk by 66%). Secondary prevention: norfloxacin.
How does portal hypertension cause examination findings?
Portal HTN (clinically significant HVPG >10mmHg): splenomegaly (splenic congestion), ascites (high sinusoidal pressure + hypoalbuminaemia + hyperaldosteronism), caput medusae (porto-systemic collaterals at umbilical vein), oesophageal varices (haemorrhage risk), haemorrhoids.
What is hepatorenal syndrome?
Functional renal failure in advanced cirrhosis — renal vasoconstriction from splanchnic vasodilation and reduced effective arterial blood volume. Type 1: rapidly progressive (creatinine doubles to >226 µmol/L in 2 weeks) — median survival weeks untreated. Type 2: slower, associated with refractory ascites. Treatment: IV albumin + terlipressin. Liver transplant is definitive.

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you distinguish hepatomegaly from other RUQ masses?
Hepatomegaly: moves with respiration, cannot get above it, dull to percussion, not ballotable. Distinguish from enlarged gallbladder (smooth, moves with respiration, more anterior — Courvoisier's) and kidney (ballotable, can get above, resonant). Combination with splenomegaly favours hepatic or systemic rather than focal mass.
What is Felty's syndrome?
Triad: RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia. Longstanding seropositive RA. Neutropenia causes increased bacterial infection susceptibility. May require splenectomy if recurrent severe infections. Associated with extra-articular RA manifestations — nodules, vasculitis, leg ulcers.
What haematological conditions cause massive splenomegaly (>8cm)?
CML (most common UK cause — Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22), BCR-ABL fusion) and myelofibrosis. Tropical: chronic malaria, visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar). Gaucher's disease (glucocerebrosidase deficiency). Hairy cell leukaemia.
What investigations would you request?
FBC + film (blast cells, lymphocytosis, thrombocytopenia). LDH (lymphoma, haemolysis). Liver screen. USS ± CT staging. Blood cultures + viral serology. Thick/thin films (malaria). Bone marrow biopsy. Lymph node biopsy for tissue diagnosis.
What is the Philadelphia chromosome?
Translocation t(9;22) creating BCR-ABL — a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. In >95% of CML. Presents with massive splenomegaly, leukocytosis with left shift, basophilia. Treated with imatinib (TKI) — transformed prognosis from 3–5 years to near-normal life expectancy.

🎓Examiner questions▶
What is Courvoisier's law?
A palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient is unlikely to be due to gallstones — more likely malignant obstruction (pancreatic head carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma). Gallstones cause chronic cholecystitis with a fibrosed, non-distensible gallbladder. It is a law of likelihood, not certainty.
What is haemochromatosis?
Autosomal recessive HFE gene mutation causing excess GI iron absorption. Males affected earlier (females protected by menstrual losses). Bronze diabetes (skin pigmentation + diabetes), hepatomegaly/cirrhosis, pseudogout arthropathy (2nd/3rd MCP characteristic), cardiac failure, hypogonadism. Investigated with ferritin, transferrin saturation, liver biopsy, HFE genotyping. Treated with regular venesection. 200× increased HCC risk if cirrhotic.
What is the significance of a hepatic bruit?
Indicates increased arterial flow — highly suspicious for HCC (hypervascular tumour). Also occurs in acute alcoholic hepatitis. Always auscultate over the liver. A bruit significantly raises pre-test probability of HCC and should prompt AFP and urgent imaging.
How does CCF cause pulsatile hepatomegaly?
Right heart failure → raised systemic venous pressure → hepatic venous congestion. TR transmits pulsation via IVC and hepatic veins into the liver — systolic pulsation is pathognomonic of TR. Chronic CCF can cause cardiac cirrhosis.
What are the surveillance and treatment options for HCC?
Surveillance: 6-monthly USS + AFP in all cirrhotics. Treatment by BCLC staging: surgical resection or transplant (Milan criteria — single ≤5cm or ≤3 nodules ≤3cm) for early disease. Locoregional therapies (TACE, RFA, SIRT) for intermediate. Systemic: sorafenib or atezolizumab/bevacizumab for advanced disease.

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you distinguish splenomegaly from a left renal mass?
Splenomegaly: moves infero-medially, medial notch, dull, cannot ballot, cannot get above. Renal mass: moves vertically, no notch, resonant (bowel overlying), ballotable bimanually, can often get above. The direction of movement and medial notch are the key differentiators.
What is hypersplenism?
Increased splenic sequestration — pancytopenia (anaemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) disproportionate to marrow function. Pallor (anaemia), purpura/bruising (thrombocytopenia), infection risk (neutropenia). Moderate portal HTN splenomegaly is sufficient to cause hypersplenism.
What vaccinations are required before splenectomy?
Ideally 2 weeks pre-op: pneumococcal (Pneumovax), meningococcal (MenACWY + MenB), Hib, influenza. If emergency — give as soon as possible post-op. Lifelong prophylactic penicillin V + medic alert bracelet. Risk from encapsulated organisms (Strep pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis).
What is the investigation pathway for new splenomegaly?
USS abdomen (confirm, measure). FBC + film. LDH (lymphoma, haemolysis). Liver screen. Blood cultures + viral serology. Thick/thin films (malaria). CT staging. Bone marrow biopsy. Lymph node biopsy for tissue diagnosis.
What is CML and how has its treatment changed?
Chronic myeloid leukaemia — Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22), BCR-ABL fusion tyrosine kinase. Presents with massive splenomegaly, leukocytosis with left shift, basophilia, thrombocytosis. Previously median survival 3–5 years. Imatinib (first-generation TKI) transformed prognosis to near-normal life expectancy. Resistant/progressive disease: dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib. Allogeneic SCT for accelerated/blast phase.

🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the top three indications for liver transplantation?
Cirrhosis (alcoholic, viral, autoimmune, metabolic). Acute hepatic failure (paracetamol overdose, hepatitis A/B, Wilson's disease). HCC within Milan criteria (single ≤5cm or ≤3 nodules ≤3cm, no vascular invasion, no extrahepatic disease). Also: PBC, PSC, metabolic liver disease (Wilson's, haemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency).
What are the Milan criteria for liver transplant in HCC?
Single tumour ≤5cm, or up to 3 nodules all ≤3cm, with no macrovascular invasion and no extrahepatic spread. Within criteria: ~70% 5-year survival, ~15% recurrence. Downstaging with locoregional therapy (TACE, ablation) may allow patients beyond criteria to qualify.
What is the graft survival following liver transplantation?
~80% at 1 year, ~70% at 5 years. Better outcomes: elective (not acute) transplant, lower Child-Pugh score, non-viral aetiology, no HCC recurrence. Alcoholic cirrhosis recipients generally required to demonstrate 6 months abstinence pre-transplant.
What causes gum hypertrophy?
Drugs: ciclosporin (most commonly seen in transplant), phenytoin, nifedipine (and other CCBs). Scurvy. Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia (gingival infiltration). Pregnancy. Familial gingival fibromatosis (rare).
What skin signs occur in transplant patients?
Malignancy: actinic keratoses (premalignant), SCC (×100), BCC (×10), melanoma (×10). Infection: viral warts (HPV), molluscum, cellulitis. All caused by impaired immunosurveillance. SCC in transplant patients behaves more aggressively — requires prompt treatment.

🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the genetics of ADPKD?
Autosomal dominant, prevalence 1:1000. ADPKD1 (chromosome 16, PKD1 — polycystin-1): 85%, earlier and more severe, end-stage by age 40–60. ADPKD2 (chromosome 4, PKD2 — polycystin-2): 15%, later and milder onset. 10% are new mutations — no family history does not exclude. Genetic counselling of all first-degree relatives over 20 years.
What are the extra-renal manifestations of ADPKD?
Hepatic cysts (~80% — most common extra-renal feature, rarely cause liver failure). Intracranial Berry aneurysms (~8% — risk of SAH). Mitral valve prolapse (~25%). Colonic diverticulosis. Seminal vesicle cysts. Pancreatic cysts (rare). Aortic root dilatation.
How does ADPKD present clinically?
Hypertension (often first sign — 60%). Haematuria (cyst rupture, stones, infection). Abdominal/flank pain (cyst haemorrhage, infection). Urinary tract infection/pyelonephritis. Abdominal mass (incidental or on examination). End-stage renal failure — average age 60 for ADPKD1.
What is tolvaptan and how does it work?
Vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist — slows cyst growth by reducing intracellular cAMP (which drives cyst epithelial proliferation and fluid secretion). TEMPO 3:4 trial showed significant slowing of total kidney volume increase and preservation of eGFR. NICE approved for rapidly progressing ADPKD (eGFR 25–60 + evidence of rapid progression). Side effects: aquaretic effects (polyuria, polydipsia) and hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs).
When do you screen for intracranial aneurysms in ADPKD?
MRA head screening for: family history of SAH or intracranial aneurysm, occupational high-risk (pilots, divers, surgeons), previous aneurysm, new severe headaches. Not recommended for all ADPKD patients due to anxiety and procedural risks. Unruptured aneurysms >7–10mm generally treated (clipping or coiling); smaller ones managed conservatively with repeat imaging.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Not applying the renal four-point safety screen — fluid status, RRT type, cause, complications
Missing the hepatomegaly — hepatic cysts are very common and the liver may be palpable
Not commenting on a craniotomy scar — represents previous Berry aneurysm, clinically highly relevant
Describing the percussion note as dull — overlying bowel makes kidneys resonant, unlike the spleen

🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the causes of ascites?
Cirrhosis (80%). Malignancy (peritoneal carcinomatosis — ovarian, gastric, colon). CCF. Nephrotic syndrome (hypoalbuminaemia). TB peritonitis. Pancreatitis. Budd-Chiari syndrome. Meigs' syndrome (ovarian fibroma + ascites + pleural effusion).
What is the SAAG and why is it useful?
Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient = serum albumin minus ascitic albumin. SAAG ≥11g/L = portal hypertension (cirrhosis, CCF, Budd-Chiari, portal vein thrombosis) — 97% accuracy. SAAG <11g/L = non-portal HTN (malignancy, TB, nephrotic, pancreatitis). Superior to old protein-based transudate/exudate classification.
How do you diagnose SBP on ascitic tap?
Ascitic neutrophil count >250 cells/mm³ — diagnostic regardless of Gram stain (often negative). Other features: low glucose, elevated LDH, positive culture (50–70%). pH <7.35. Treat with IV cefotaxime + albumin (1.5g/kg at diagnosis, 1g/kg at day 3 — prevents HRS by 66%). Secondary prevention: norfloxacin.
What is hepatorenal syndrome?
Functional renal failure in advanced cirrhosis — renal vasoconstriction from splanchnic vasodilation and reduced effective arterial blood volume. Type 1: rapidly progressive (creatinine doubles to >226 µmol/L within 2 weeks) — weeks survival untreated. Type 2: slower, associated with refractory ascites. Treatment: IV albumin + terlipressin. Liver transplant definitive.

🎓Examiner questions▶
What are the extra-intestinal manifestations of IBD?
Related to disease activity: erythema nodosum, peripheral arthropathy (large joint, non-erosive), uveitis/episcleritis. Independent: ankylosing spondylitis, sacroiliitis, pyoderma gangrenosum, PSC (particularly UC). Other: aphthous ulcers, clubbing, gallstones (Crohn's — ileal disease, reduced bile salt reabsorption), renal stones (oxalate — steatorrhoea).
What is the association between UC and PSC?
~70% of PSC patients have UC. Only 5% of UC patients develop PSC, but the combination significantly increases colorectal cancer risk (annual colonoscopy from PSC diagnosis). PSC: independent risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma. Treatment of UC does not alter PSC course — liver transplant is the only treatment for PSC.
What is the medical management of Crohn's?
Induction: corticosteroids (prednisolone, budesonide for ileocolonic). Maintenance: azathioprine/mercaptopurine or methotrexate. Biologics: anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab) for moderate-severe/steroid-refractory; vedolizumab (anti-integrin); ustekinumab (anti-IL12/23). Nutritional therapy: elemental diet — especially in children. Smoking cessation: single most important lifestyle intervention.
What are the complications of Crohn's?
Strictures (obstruction). Fistulae (enteroenteric, enterovesical, enterocutaneous, perianal). Abscess. Malabsorption (terminal ileal disease — B12, bile salts, fat-soluble vitamins). Short bowel syndrome (post-resection). Small bowel SCC (rare). Osteoporosis (steroids + malabsorption). Amyloidosis (longstanding, rare).
How does IBD relate to bronchiectasis?
IBD (particularly UC) is a recognised cause of bronchiectasis — immune-mediated cross-reactivity between gut and airway epithelium. Listed in the immune-overactivity category. Patients with IBD may develop lower respiratory tract disease even in clinical remission. An important link between the abdominal and respiratory PACES cases.

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you distinguish expansile from transmitted pulsation?
Place both hands on either side of the mass. Expansile: hands pushed apart laterally with each pulsation — indicates aneurysm (AAA). Transmitted: both hands move together — mass overlying a normal aorta (e.g. lymph node). A tender or rapidly expanding AAA is a vascular emergency.
How would you investigate a new abdominal mass?
Bloods: FBC, LFTs, U&E, LDH, AFP (HCC/testicular), CA125 (ovarian), CEA (colonic), beta-hCG (testicular/gestational). USS abdomen (first-line). CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast (characterise, staging). Tissue diagnosis: CT-guided biopsy, colonoscopy, endoscopy, laparoscopy. MDT discussion before biopsy if resectable — avoid seeding the tract.
What is the significance of a non-tender epigastric expansile mass?
AAA — defined as aortic diameter >3cm. Clinically significant if >5.5cm in men (>5.0cm in women) or rapidly expanding (>1cm/year). Elective repair: EVAR or open surgery. Urgent repair if tender (imminent rupture) or ruptured (haemodynamic instability — mortality 80%). UK national AAA screening programme: one-time USS to men aged 65.
What tumour markers are useful?
AFP: HCC (>400ng/mL diagnostic in context), testicular non-seminoma. CEA: colorectal cancer (monitoring). CA125: ovarian cancer (monitoring — also elevated in endometriosis, peritonitis, CCF). PSA: prostate. Beta-hCG: gestational trophoblastic disease, testicular teratoma. LDH: lymphoma, haemolysis (non-specific but prognostic).
What features suggest malignancy?
Hard/firm consistency. Irregular, craggy surface. Non-tender (malignant masses often painless until late). Fixed to surrounding structures. Cachexia and weight loss. Lymphadenopathy. Signs of metastatic disease (hepatomegaly, ascites, pleural effusion). Raised tumour markers. Contrast with inflammatory masses (Crohn's, abscess) — tender, softer.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Naming the diagnosis before characterising the mass — always describe fully first
Confusing expansile from transmitted pulsation — this distinction diagnoses AAA
Not offering testicular examination in males — retroperitoneal masses may be metastatic from testicular primary
Not looking for lymphadenopathy — changes differential toward haematological or metastatic disease
Missing cachexia — always comment on nutritional status; shifts differential toward malignancy
