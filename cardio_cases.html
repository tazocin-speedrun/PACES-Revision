Case 01 · Most Common · 17.2%

Metallic Aortic Valve Replacement

17.2% — most common Station 3 Stem: dyspnoea / chest pain / syncope
⚡ Spot diagnosis — before you touch the patient
Audible metallic click on approach Median sternotomy scar Bruising from warfarin Pacemaker scar possible Marfan's / AS / RA features
🔎Full examination — findings for this case▶
1
End of bed
Median sternotomy scar. Audible metallic click — often heard before stethoscope applied. Bruising from warfarin. Infraclavicular pacemaker scar if device present.
2
Hands / nails
IE screen Splinter haemorrhages, clubbing. Bruising / petechiae from anticoagulation. Pulse: usually regular — AF = complication / IE.
3
Face / eyes
Underlying cause: Marfan's (high-arched palate, lens dislocation), ankylosing spondylitis, RA. Anaemia (haemolysis from valve). Xanthelasma (atherosclerotic).
4
JVP
Usually normal if valve functioning well. Raised JVP = RVF / decompensation.
5
Carotid pulse
Normal character expected post-AVR. Collapsing pulse = residual / recurrent AR = valve dysfunction.
6
Precordium
Sternotomy scar. Apex: normal position expected if functioning well. Pacemaker device infraclavicular if present. No heave expected.
7
Auscultation — MITRAL FIRST
Mitral: Normal S1 expected — no MR in isolated AVR.

Aortic: Loud metallic closing click = replaces A2, heard all over precordium. Soft ESM (flow murmur) = normal post-AVR. Opening click in early systole possible.

Abnormal: Quieter click = clot / vegetation. New EDM (lean forward + expiration) = paraprosthetic AR = dysfunction / IE.
8
Lung bases / ankles / BP
Clear bases expected if well. Bibasal crackles = LVF from valve dysfunction. Ankle oedema = RVF. BP both arms.
💬Ideal presentation script▶
1 — State your diagnosis
"I think this patient has a metallic aortic valve replacement."
2 — Positive signs supporting this
"The positive signs supporting this are: a loud metallic closing click heard all over the precordium coinciding with S2, a soft ejection systolic flow murmur in the aortic area, and a median sternotomy scar. The valve appears to be functioning normally — the click is of good intensity and there is no early diastolic murmur to suggest paraprosthetic regurgitation."
If underlying cause visible: "The underlying aetiology appears to be [Marfan's / bicuspid valve / RA] based on [specific signs]."
3 — Important negatives (fixed safety screen — every time)
"Important negatives: there are no peripheral stigmata of infective endocarditis. The patient is in sinus rhythm. There are no signs of left ventricular failure — breath sounds are clear at the bases. There are no signs of right ventricular failure — JVP is not raised and there is no peripheral oedema. There is no RV heave and P2 is not accentuated."
4 — Of note but less relevant
"Of note, there is evidence of anticoagulation — bruising — consistent with warfarin therapy for the mechanical valve."

🔬Investigations and management▶
Investigations
ECG — rhythm, AF, LVH
Echo — gradient, paraprosthetic leak, vegetations, LV function
CXR — valve ring opacity, pulmonary oedema
FBC — haemolytic anaemia (↑LDH, reticulocytes)
INR — target 2.5–3.5 for metallic AVR
Blood cultures ×3 if IE suspected
Fluoroscopy — leaflet motion if dysfunction queried
Management / key points
Lifelong warfarin — INR 2.5–3.5
Good dental hygiene essential
Metal = durable, needs warfarin
Tissue = no warfarin, less durable (~10yr)
Operative mortality 3–5%
Thromboembolism 1–2%/yr despite warfarin
Prosthetic valve IE mortality ~60%
Early IE: Staph epidermidis (skin)
Late IE: Strep viridans (haematogenous)
Haemolysis: mechanical RBC destruction
🎓Examiner questions and model answers▶
What are the indications for aortic valve replacement?
Severe AS (gradient >40mmHg, valve area <1cm²) with symptoms (angina, syncope, dyspnoea). Severe AR with symptoms or declining LV function (EF <50% or dilating LV). Severe AS undergoing other cardiac surgery.
Why does this patient need warfarin?
Metallic valves are thrombogenic. Warfarin reduces thromboembolism risk — target INR 2.5–3.5 for AVR. Tissue valves do not require long-term warfarin unless another indication (e.g. AF).
What are the late complications of prosthetic valve replacement?
Thromboembolism (1–2%/yr), bleeding (fatal 0.6%, major 3%/yr), haemolysis, IE (early = Staph epidermidis; late = Strep viridans), bioprosthetic dysfunction ~10yr, AF (especially MVR), paravalvular regurgitation.
How would you identify valve dysfunction on examination?
Quieter metallic click (clot / vegetation reducing leaflet excursion), new EDM (paraprosthetic AR), new PSM, new signs of heart failure, peripheral IE stigmata. Any change in click character is significant.
Metal vs tissue valve — how do you decide?
Metal: young patients, already on warfarin, durable. Tissue: elderly, high bleeding risk, women considering pregnancy — no warfarin needed but less durable (~10yr, can be replaced with valve-in-valve TAVI).
The patient needs surgery — what about their warfarin?
Bridging anticoagulation — stop warfarin 5 days pre-op, bridge with LMWH. Restart post-op once haemostasis established. Metallic AVR is high thrombotic risk — bridging usually recommended. Joint decision with haematology and cardiology.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Confusing AVR (click = S2) with MVR (click = S1) — time the click carefully against the apex beat
Missing a quiet click — listen on approach; metallic click often audible from the doorway
Not commenting on whether the valve appears functioning normally — always state click intensity and whether any new murmur is present
Forgetting the IE screen — prosthetic valve IE is high stakes and examiners always probe this specifically
Calling the soft ESM pathological — a flow murmur is entirely normal post-AVR and does not indicate dysfunction
Not looking for the underlying cause — always assess for Marfan's, ankylosing spondylitis, RA, bicuspid valve stigmata

Comfortable or breathless (severe). Sternotomy scar if post-AVR (tissue valve now). Xanthelasma visible.
2

Slow-rising, low-volume, plateau pulse — key severity marker. The slower the rise, the more severe. Note rhythm — AF = complication.

5

Usually normal. Raised = RVF (severe late disease).
6

Heaving, non-displaced apex — pressure-loaded LV. Systolic thrill in aortic area (severe). No parasternal heave unless pulmonary HTN.
7
Auscultation — MITRAL FIRST
Bell at apex: S4 — stiff non-compliant LV, key severity marker. Check for MR.

Diaphragm at aortic area: Harsh ESM crescendo-decrescendo, 2nd ICS RSE. Radiates to carotids. Soft/absent A2 — severity marker. Delayed peak = more severe.

Lean forward + expiration, lower LSE: always exclude coexistent AR.
8
BP / completions
Narrow pulse pressure. Bibasal crackles (severe LVF). Ankle oedema.

These findings suggest [moderate / severe] aortic stenosis."
3
"Important negatives: no IE stigmata. Sinus rhythm. No LVF — bases clear. No RVF. No pulmonary hypertension. No early diastolic murmur to suggest coexistent AR."
4
"Of note, there is xanthelasma suggesting an atherosclerotic aetiology."

🎓Examiner questions▶
Cardinal symptoms and their significance?
Angina (3yr survival without intervention), syncope (3yr), dyspnoea / heart failure (1–2yr). Symptom onset = critical turning point — AVR / TAVI indicated once symptoms develop.
How do you assess severity clinically?
Slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure, soft/absent A2, delayed ESM peak, heaving apex, S4, LVF signs. Murmur loudness does NOT correlate — severe AS in low-output state can have a soft or absent murmur.
Indications for intervention?
Severe AS + symptoms. EF <50%. Severe AS undergoing other cardiac surgery. TAVI for high / intermediate surgical risk. SAVR for lower-risk, younger patients.
What is TAVI?
Transcatheter aortic valve implantation — percutaneous replacement via femoral artery, avoiding sternotomy. Used in high / intermediate surgical risk. Increasingly used in younger, lower-risk patients at experienced centres.
Causes of AS?
Calcific degeneration (most common, elderly). Bicuspid aortic valve (most common in <70yr — congenitally bicuspid valves calcify earlier). Rheumatic (rare in UK — associated with MS).
✗Common pitfalls▶
Judging severity by murmur loudness — severe low-output AS often has a soft murmur
Missing the slow-rising pulse — feel the carotid, not just the radial
Not excluding AR — always lean forward and listen at lower LSE in expiration
Forgetting S4 on bell — key severity marker, easily missed without bell at apex
Not grading severity — always commit to mild / moderate / severe with supporting evidence

1

2
"The positive signs are: a displaced, thrusting apex in the [X]th ICS consistent with LV volume overload, a soft S1, a pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla accentuated in the left lateral position, and an S3 on bell auscultation. These findings suggest [moderate / severe] mitral regurgitation."
3

🎓Examiner questions▶
Causes of MR?
Primary: MVP (most common in West), rheumatic, IE, papillary muscle rupture (post-MI), connective tissue (Marfan's). Secondary / functional: dilated cardiomyopathy with annular dilatation — LV dilates and pulls leaflets apart.
When to intervene?
Severe MR + symptoms. EF <60% or LVESD >45mm even asymptomatic — LV decompensating. Repair preferred over replacement — better LV function preservation, no anticoagulation.
Why is EF <60% the threshold in MR?
MR allows LV to eject into low-pressure LA — EF is artificially elevated. EF 60% in MR ≈ EF 40% normally. EF <60% therefore indicates significant underlying LV dysfunction.
Acute vs chronic MR?
Acute (papillary rupture post-MI, IE): flash pulmonary oedema, cardiogenic shock on non-dilated LV — emergency surgery. Chronic: LV dilates gradually, compensated for years before symptoms develop.

Displaced, thrusting, hyperdynamic apex — volume-loaded LV. May be 6th ICS anterior axillary line in severe. No parasternal heave.

Wide pulse pressure on BP. Lung bases. Mention Duroziez's sign (femoral) to complete.
💬Presentation script▶
1
"I think this patient has aortic regurgitation."
2
"The positive signs are: a collapsing pulse with a wide pulse pressure of [X/Y], a displaced thrusting apex in the [X]th ICS, and an early diastolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge heard best leaning forward in expiration. There is an S3. These suggest [moderate / severe] AR with a volume-loaded, dilated LV."
If Austin-Flint present: "There is also a mid-diastolic murmur at the apex on bell — an Austin-Flint murmur — consistent with severe AR."
3
"Important negatives: no IE stigmata. Sinus rhythm. No LVF — bases clear. No RVF. No pulmonary hypertension. There are no features of Marfan's syndrome or ankylosing spondylitis on inspection."
Marfan's / AS exclusion is a disease-specific negative for AR — always include it here.
🎓Examiner questions▶
Causes of AR?
Valve: bicuspid aortic valve, IE, rheumatic, myxomatous. Aortic root dilatation: Marfan's, ankylosing spondylitis, RA, syphilitic aortitis, hypertension, aortic dissection.
When to intervene?
Severe AR + symptoms. EF <50%. LVESD >50mm (or >25mm/m² BSA) even asymptomatic — LV dilating = decompensation imminent.
What is the Austin-Flint murmur?
MDM at apex in severe AR — regurgitant jet impinges on the anterior mitral leaflet causing functional MS. Distinguished from true MS by: no opening snap, normal/soft S1, wide pulse pressure, collapsing pulse.
Eponymous signs in AR?
All reflect wide pulse pressure / hyperdynamic circulation: Corrigan's (carotid pulsation), De Musset's (head nodding), Quincke's (nail bed pulsation), Duroziez's (femoral bruit), Traube's (pistol shot femoral), Müller's (uvula pulsation).

🎓Examiner questions▶
How do you distinguish AVR from MVR?
AVR: click replaces A2 (S2) — heard all over, soft ESM flow murmur. MVR: click replaces S1 — best at apex with lateral radiation, opening click in diastole, diastolic rumble with bell.
INR target for MVR?
INR 3.0–4.0 — higher than AVR (2.5–3.5) due to greater thromboembolism risk in the mitral position (lower flow velocity).
Why is AF so common after MVR?
Longstanding MS causes LA dilatation before MVR. LA dilatation persists post-operatively. AF is often permanent — rate control + anticoagulation is the usual management.

🎓Examiner questions▶
Most common cause?
Rheumatic heart disease (>90%). Commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, subvalvular apparatus fibrosis. Rare: congenital, carcinoid, SLE.
Treatment options?
Medical: rate control (AF), anticoagulation (high stroke risk), diuretics. Intervention: PMBV (if anatomy suitable — no MR, no LA clot) or surgical MVR. PMBV preferred if anatomy favourable. Echo: valve area <1cm² = severe.
Why does MS cause haemoptysis?
Elevated LA pressure → pulmonary venous HTN → pulmonary oedema / rupture of bronchial veins. Frank haemoptysis = Winterbottom haemoptysis from bronchial vein rupture.

Using diaphragm for MDM — always use bell at apex in left lateral position in expiration
Missing the opening snap — listen carefully just after A2
Not correlating A2-OS interval with severity — shorter = more severe
Forgetting that AF abolishes presystolic accentuation

🎓Examiner questions▶
Causes of AF?
Cardiac: hypertension (most common), IHD, mitral valve disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy. Non-cardiac: thyrotoxicosis, alcohol (holiday heart), PE, pneumonia, electrolyte disturbance. Lone AF (no cause, young).
Stroke risk and anticoagulation?
CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. Anticoagulate if ≥2 (men) or ≥3 (women). DOACs preferred unless valvular AF (mechanical valve / MS) — use warfarin. Assess bleeding risk with HAS-BLED.
Rate vs rhythm control?
Rate control for most — equivalent outcomes (AFFIRM trial). Rhythm control for: younger, symptomatic despite rate control, first presentation, clear precipitant. Always anticoagulate 3 weeks before cardioversion or TOE to exclude LA clot.
CHA₂DS₂-VASc score?
CCF(1), Hypertension(1), Age≥75(2), Diabetes(1), Stroke/TIA(2), Vascular disease(1), Age 65–74(1), Sex female(1). Maximum 9.
Case 08 · 5.0%

Mitral Valve Prolapse

5.0%Station 3 Stem: palpitations / incidental murmur
⚡ Spot diagnosis
Mid-systolic click at apexLate systolic murmur after click Young, often femaleMarfan's / pectus excavatum Click moves earlier on standing
🔎Full examination — findings▶
1
End of bed
Often young female. Marfan's (tall, long limbs, high-arched palate), Ehlers-Danlos (hypermobile joints, skin elasticity), pectus excavatum, straight back syndrome.
2
Pulse / JVP
Usually regular. JVP normal.
3
Precordium
Apex: normal position unless significant MR has developed. No heave or thrill typically.
4
Auscultation — ★ Dynamic manoeuvres essential
Mid-systolic click at apex — non-ejection, after S1. Late systolic murmur after click (MR from prolapsing leaflet).

Standing from squatting: click moves earlier, murmur longer (smaller LV = earlier prolapse).
Squatting: click moves later, murmur shorter (larger LV = later prolapse).
Valsalva strain: click moves earlier (same mechanism as standing).

If significant MR developed: PSM, displaced apex.
💬Presentation script▶
1
"I think this patient has mitral valve prolapse."
2
"The positive signs are: a mid-systolic click at the apex followed by a late systolic murmur. On standing from squatting, the click moved earlier and the murmur lengthened — consistent with MVP. The apex is not displaced, suggesting no significant LV volume overload."
If Marfan's features: "There are features of Marfan's syndrome — the likely underlying aetiology."
3
"Important negatives: no IE stigmata. Sinus rhythm. No LVF or RVF. No pulmonary hypertension. No displaced apex to suggest significant MR has developed."
🎓Examiner questions▶
Mechanism of the click?
As LV contracts, elongated chordae reach maximum tension and prolapsing leaflets billow into the LA — this tensioning creates the click. Smaller LV (standing) = earlier click.
Complications of MVP?
Progressive MR (most important — can require surgery). IE. Arrhythmias. Cerebral emboli (rare). Chordal rupture (acute severe MR).
Why does standing make the murmur longer?
Standing → ↓ venous return → smaller LV → earlier prolapse → click earlier → murmur occupies more of systole. Squatting has the opposite effect.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Not performing dynamic manoeuvres — MVP can be missed or mistaken without standing / Valsalva
Calling the click an ejection click — mid-systolic (after S1), not early systolic
Not commenting on whether significant MR has developed — displaced apex and S3 suggest this

How do you determine the dominant lesion?
Pulse: slow-rising = AS dominant; collapsing = AR dominant. Pulse pressure: narrow = AS; wide = AR. Apex: heaving non-displaced = AS; displaced thrusting = AR. Echo gradient and valve area is definitive.
Why is mixed disease significant?

🎓Examiner questions▶
What is Eisenmenger's syndrome?
Reversal of left-to-right shunt due to progressive pulmonary HTN. As pulmonary vascular resistance exceeds systemic, flow reverses (right-to-left) → cyanosis, clubbing. Murmur diminishes. Surgical correction then contraindicated — the shunt is maintaining cardiac output.
Why does a small VSD have a louder murmur?
Maladie de Roger — a small restrictive VSD maintains a high LV-to-RV pressure gradient → high-velocity turbulent flow → loud murmur. Large VSD equalises pressures → quieter murmur.
When do you close a VSD?
Large VSD with Qp:Qs >1.5:1, LV volume overload, pulmonary HTN before Eisenmenger's. Small VSDs often close spontaneously in childhood. Closure contraindicated in established Eisenmenger's.
✗Common pitfalls▶
Confusing VSD with MR — VSD has no axillary radiation and thrill at lower LSE, not apex
Not checking for cyanosis — Eisenmenger's changes management completely
Not checking P2 — pulmonary HTN from a large shunt is a key finding
Expecting a loud murmur to mean less severe — a small VSD is louder than a large one
